# Methods and uncertainty

Use when inference, comparisons, associations or uncertainty are requested. Input: a defined estimand, eligible evidence and sampling/assignment structure. Result: an appropriate descriptive or inferential analysis with a documented scope, not an automatic significance test.

## Establish the design first

1. State the target quantity in natural units: mean difference, risk difference, rate ratio, distributional contrast, correlation, or another defined estimand. Identify the target population and the actual sampling/assignment process. Distinguish a census of the observed window from a sample used to generalize to other people or times; a census can still have coverage/measurement error.
2. Identify independent units and related observations: same entity before/after, repeated visits, households/sites, overlapping windows or time series. Record weights and their meaning. Row count is not necessarily the independent sample size.
3. Inspect group sizes, missing outcomes, selection, distribution/tails, ties, variance, outliers, exposure and temporal patterns. Assess assumptions for the proposed estimator/test, not an arbitrary checklist. Failure to detect nonnormality does not prove normality, particularly in small samples. Do not choose a test solely because a preliminary assumption test crossed a threshold.
4. For confirmatory work, specify hypotheses, sidedness, confidence level, error criterion, primary outcome and comparison family before looking for favorable results. Otherwise label the work exploratory. Do not invent a fixed business importance threshold; ask the decision context or report effect sizes without declaring materiality.

## Choose a branch

| Evidence and question | Candidate analysis | Conditions and failure branch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Describe only the observed population/window | Counts, rates, mean/median, quantiles, subgroup distributions | Carry coverage and missingness. A confidence interval is not compulsory for a descriptive census; do not invent sampling uncertainty. |
| Mean difference, two independent groups | Welch two-sample t estimate/test and matching interval | Independent units and a defensible sampling distribution of the means; inspect tails/influential observations and small samples. Do not assume equal variances just for convenience. Severe instability calls for a justified robust/resampling approach or descriptive limits, not automatic deletion. |
| Same units measured twice or deliberately matched pairs | Analyze within-pair differences; paired t inference if appropriate | Verify pair identity, direction and missing pairs. Assumptions concern the differences and independence between pairs, not independence within a pair. Report complete pairs and missing-pair selection. Never reconstruct pairs by row position. |
| One binary proportion | Proportion plus Wilson or suitable exact binomial interval | Defined successes/eligible trials and independent binomial trials; document confidence level and method. Avoid a naive symmetric normal interval at sparse or boundary counts. Zero successes with positive n does not prove zero population risk. Zero eligible trials supplies no proportion or binomial interval. |
| Two independent binary groups or categorical association | Risk difference/ratio or odds ratio as appropriate, with a compatible interval; contingency-table test if requested | Preserve cell counts and the table orientation. Check adequacy of a chi-square approximation; for sparse 2×2 tables consider a Fisher exact test under its fixed-marginal null. It does not automatically provide the desired risk-difference interval. Paired binary observations need a paired method, not an independent-table test. |
| Ordinal outcomes or a distributional contrast between independent groups | Rank-based comparison such as Mann–Whitney, with an interpretable effect summary | Independence remains necessary; check ties and exact/asymptotic method support. Mann–Whitney is not generically a test of equal medians; a location/median interpretation needs additional shape assumptions. If the target is a mean, switching to ranks changes the question. |
| More than two groups, several contrasts or covariate adjustment | Design-appropriate model/omnibus analysis plus planned contrasts | Define the contrast and dependence/variance assumptions. Report multiplicity handling and effect intervals. An omnibus result does not identify the differing groups; a nonsignificant result does not establish equality. Complex modeling needs its own justified specification, not a default fit. |
| Time trends, repeated entities, clusters or surveys | Dependence-aware estimate/interval or a design-preserving analysis | Consider cluster-robust, repeated-measures, survey-design or time-series methods only with the required design information and adequate independent units. Do not apply ordinary independent-row standard errors or bootstrap rows across dependencies. If design support is absent, report descriptive results and name the missing prerequisite. |

For any candidate requiring unavailable software, release-specific syntax or design expertise, provide the exact proposed method and missing prerequisite rather than fabricated output. Supplied SAS/SQL/pandas summaries are acceptable evidence only for what their fields, filters and design actually support. A mean alone cannot provide a variance or a paired-difference uncertainty estimate.

## Quantify and interpret uncertainty

1. Report the estimate/effect in meaningful units before the p-value. Include group counts, interval level and method, and denominator/exposure. Distinguish standard deviation (observation spread), standard error (estimator variability), confidence interval (parameter uncertainty under assumptions), and prediction interval (future observation uncertainty).
2. For independent observations, a mean's t interval has form `mean ± t_(1−α/2,n−1) * s/sqrt(n)` with `n > 1` and justified distributional assumptions. The paired version uses the mean and standard deviation of within-pair differences and the number of complete independent pairs. Do not substitute a count of visits or pooled pre/post variation. For Welch inference use the unequal-variance standard error and appropriate degrees of freedom, not the pooled-variance formula.
3. A frequentist confidence level describes long-run coverage of the procedure under assumptions, not the probability that this fixed interval contains the parameter. A p-value concerns data as or more extreme under the specified null/model; it is neither the probability the null is true nor the probability the result is practically important. Nonsignificance is not evidence of equivalence; equivalence needs a justified margin and an appropriate design/analysis.
4. For bootstrap inference, justify the estimator, resampling unit, interval method, repetitions and seed; preserve pairs/clusters/time structure as applicable. Ordinary resampling is not assumption-free and cannot repair selection bias or unobserved tails. Assess numerical stability rather than treating a fixed repetition count as universally sufficient. Permutation methods similarly require an exchangeability/assignment justification.
5. For multiple outcomes/groups, repeated looks or selected subgroups, define the comparison family and control criterion. Use a justified familywise-error procedure (for example Bonferroni for a specified family) or a documented false-discovery procedure when that is the goal; report adjusted results and exploration separately. Repeatedly testing until significant invalidates a fixed-look claim unless the design accounts for it.
6. Sensitivity analyses should address real uncertainty: missing-outcome assumptions, plausible outliers, definition choices, composition and dependence. Label analyses planned versus post hoc and show whether the decision changes. A precise interval can coexist with serious systematic bias.

## Bound the conclusion

Randomized assignment, sampling and observational comparisons are different evidence designs. Association, adjusted association, correlation and a before/after difference do not alone identify a causal effect. A causal claim needs a justified identification strategy, assumptions and diagnostics; if these are absent, use observational language and discuss confounding, selection, seasonality and measurement changes. Do not quietly add model training to answer an analysis request.

Finish with `estimand | design/independent unit | method/version if executed | assumptions/diagnostics | estimate and interval | test/multiplicity if applicable | sensitivity | scope of conclusion`. Mark unavailable assumptions/evidence and withhold unsupported inferential precision while retaining useful descriptive results.
