# Calculation checks

Use when numerical claims depend on queries, aggregates, joins or period comparisons. Inspect supplied material; run local calculations only under the entrypoint's authorization boundary.

1. **Define the expected quantity first.** Write the intended population, unit, grain, formula, units, weights, null policy and rounding rule. Distinguish a mean per user from a mean per transaction or a mean across groups. Derive a small independent expected result from source rows or reconcilable sufficient summaries. If the denominator or overlap is unknown, mark the calculation unverified rather than inventing counts.
2. **Trace the transformation.** Identify inputs and snapshot/revision, join keys, expected cardinality, filters, deduplication rule and aggregation order. Compare key uniqueness, unmatched keys, row counts, distinct analytical units and conserved totals before/after each relevant join when evidence is supplied. A one-to-many join may be intended; the defect is counting replicated measures at the wrong grain. `DISTINCT` or a later average does not by itself prove a fix. Without cardinality evidence, flag the specific multiplication risk as unverified, not an observed duplicate count.
3. **Reconcile summaries.** Check totals against components, weighted versus unweighted averages, and ratios of totals versus averages of ratios. For disjoint groups with positive frequency counts, pooled mean = sum(group count × group mean) / sum(group count). A simple mean of group means answers a different, equal-group-weight question. Survey, exposure or analytic weights need their stated estimand and variance method; do not treat all weights as replication counts. Check missing values in numerator and denominator together and whether excluded rows change the target population.
4. **Inspect comparisons over time.** Reconcile start/end inclusivity, timezone and daylight-saving effects, event time versus processing time, complete versus partial periods, fiscal/calendar alignment, late arrivals and revisions. Missing periods are not automatically zero. Check stable cohort versus changing membership, denominator shifts and changes in measurement definitions. Compare like windows before accepting a growth claim.
5. **Check arithmetic and domain meaning.** For a positive nonzero baseline, relative change = (new − old) / old; percentage-point change is the subtraction of percentages, not that relative change. A zero baseline makes this relative change undefined. A negative baseline needs an explicit domain-approved interpretation rather than an automatic growth label. Negative net revenue can reflect returns or adjustments; growth above 100% can be valid. Reject values only against an evidenced definition, not a universal sign/range heuristic. Retain full precision until the documented reporting step and reconcile rounding discrepancies to a stated tolerance.
6. **Record coverage.** For each material metric, report reported value, independent expected value/expression, difference and units, source location, assumptions and status: recalculated from supplied data, checked from supplied summaries, inspected only, or unverified with reason. Do not equate agreement with proof that the source is complete.

## Original synthetic example: unequal groups

Definition: mean spend per user across two disjoint groups in the same window, all users included and each counted once. Supplied group summaries: A has 100 users and mean spend 50 currency units; B has 10 users and mean spend 200.

- Expected total: 100 × 50 + 10 × 200 = 7,000.
- Expected count: 110; pooled mean: 7,000 / 110 = 63.63636… currency units per user, or 63.64 to two decimals.
- Reported 125 = (50 + 200) / 2 is an equal-group mean, not the specified per-user mean. The discrepancy is about 61.36 currency units per user after rounding; verify the unrounded values and stated precision before reporting an exact difference.
- Evidence can verify the pooling arithmetic from these summaries. It cannot verify the underlying group membership or source-row completeness without their evidence. If the intended metric were an equal-group average, 125 would not be the same defect.

The weighted-mean formula is supported by NIST's Dataplot reference; the review sequence and example are independently authored. Exact source and limits are in [../SOURCES.md](../SOURCES.md).
