# Bias and inference checks

Use when an analysis compares groups, tests a hypothesis, estimates uncertainty, predicts outcomes or claims an explanation. Review the actual design and claims; do not substitute a new model or run unapproved experiments.

1. **Locate the estimand and design.** Identify the target population, outcome, exposure/intervention, comparison, observation period, assignment mechanism and analytical unit. Separate descriptive association, prediction and causal effect. Ask whether the evidence supports the population named in the headline, not only the observed sample.
2. **Trace selection and attrition.** Inspect inclusion/exclusion, sampling frame, nonresponse, missingness and retention by group and period. Check whether failed, closed, churned or otherwise unavailable entities were dropped, producing survivorship bias. Post-exposure filtering or selecting on outcomes can alter the comparison. Record plausible direction only when justified; unknown selection does not prove a bias of a particular magnitude. Missingness thresholds require a domain and consequence, not a universal cutoff.
3. **Check dependencies.** Determine whether observations are paired, repeated within people, clustered by site/household, overlapping across groups or serially correlated. Compare the unit of assignment, observation and analysis; row count is not necessarily independent sample size. Check that the reported uncertainty method accounts for the relevant design. For paired observations, inspect matching and differences rather than accepting an independent-groups calculation. If only group means are supplied, uncertainty may be uncheckable without sample sizes, variability and covariance/design information.
4. **Check time ordering.** Ask what information was available at each decision/prediction time. Inspect future outcomes in predictors, post-outcome cohort definitions, retrospective revisions and train/evaluation overlap or repeated-entity leakage. A transformation fitted on all periods can contaminate an evaluation. Distinguish source timestamp from actual availability; without this evidence, temporal validity remains unverified.
5. **Review statistical evidence.** Bind the test/model, null hypothesis, assumptions, sample sizes, effect estimate and units, interval type/level and method. Check missing-data handling, planned versus exploratory analyses, number of outcomes/subgroups/windows/tests and selection of reported results. Ask for multiplicity handling or an explicit exploratory limitation; do not select an adjustment blindly. A p-value is not a probability that the null is true, an effect size, or a probability of practical importance. Threshold crossing alone does not justify a decision; non-significance does not establish equivalence or absence of an effect. Check interval interpretation and whether uncertainty covers sampling only or also other material errors. Do not fabricate a confidence interval from a p-value alone.
6. **Challenge causal wording.** Inspect assignment, confounding control, pre/post trends, concurrent changes, spillovers, attrition and any identification assumptions relevant to the claimed design. Random assignment is useful evidence, not a reason to ignore implementation failures; observational causal designs require explicit defensible assumptions. Association or a before/after increase alone cannot establish that an intervention caused the change. Recommend a supported descriptive wording or request the design evidence that could justify causality, rather than declaring that no causal effect exists.
7. **State the consequence.** Tie each supported issue or material gap to the decision: inflated certainty, non-generalizable population, contaminated future performance, or attribution not established. Distinguish a demonstrated method/wording defect from an unverified assumption, and name the evidence needed to resolve it.

## Original synthetic example: unsupported attribution

Supplied report, conclusion paragraph: “The new onboarding caused a 20% increase in retention.” Its only evidence is a before/after chart; no concurrent comparison, assignment/design record or confounding analysis is supplied.

Finding: the causal attribution is unsupported by the inspected evidence. Do not assert that the increase is false or that onboarding had no effect. The 20% calculation itself remains unverified unless its cohorts, numerator/denominator and windows are supplied and checked. Decision impact: committing resources on the claimed intervention effect may mistake seasonal or composition changes for impact. Correction/check: use a descriptive increase limited to the observed population if that arithmetic is verified, or provide a credible identification design and its assumptions, implementation and uncertainty evidence before retaining causal wording.
